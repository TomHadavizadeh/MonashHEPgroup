In the Standard Model the three charged leptons (the electron, the muon and
the tau) each keep their own "flavour". A muon can decay, but it should never
turn directly into an electron without emitting neutrinos. Many theories of
physics beyond the Standard Model, including supersymmetric models, predict
that this *charged lepton flavour violation* does occur at a small rate. Seeing
it would be clear evidence of new physics.

**COMET** (COherent Muon-to-Electron Transition) is being built at the J-PARC
accelerator complex in Tokai, Japan. An intense beam of muons is stopped in
a thin target, where the muons are captured by nuclei. COMET looks for the
single, mono-energetic electron that would be emitted if a captured muon
converted directly into an electron. The experiment aims to improve on the
sensitivity of previous searches by a factor of about 10,000.

## What we do at Monash

The Monash group is responsible for the main part of COMET's trigger
electronics, as well as software development and simulation studies. The
trigger decides, in real time, which of the many millions of beam
interactions are worth recording. This makes it one of the most important
parts of a rare-process search.

Students working on COMET get hands-on experience with:

- trigger hardware and FPGA firmware,
- detector simulation with Geant4,
- analysis techniques for rare-event searches.
